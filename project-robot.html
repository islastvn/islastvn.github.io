UVic · ENGR 120/121 · P-06

Autonomous
Search-and-Place Robot

Overview

This project was set by Ocean Networks Canada as a scaled, dry-lab stand-in for a real problem: underwater observatories off the BC coast lose data when debris and bio-fouling settle on their sensors, and an autonomous robot is needed to clear them. Our task was to build a small robot that, with no human control, searches a 2.29 m by 2.29 m arena, locates a flashing infrared beacon standing in for the sensor, and places an object on top of it to simulate the cleaning action, then retreats and signals that it is done.

I worked on a team and led the electronics and software. The robot finds the target by turning a full circle to map where the infrared signal is strongest, drives toward it, re-checks its heading partway in, and closes to a fixed 5 cm standoff before placing the object and backing away.

The Challenge

The robot had to run fully autonomously from any starting position and orientation the lab chose, which made the search the hard part: it begins knowing nothing about where the target is. On top of that, the rules rewarded behaviour that would matter on a real sea floor, so the design had to earn marks on more than just finishing.

  • Find a 10 Hz flashing IR beacon somewhere in the arena, from an unknown starting heading.
  • Place a ball cleanly on the target without knocking it over, and have it stay put.
  • Avoid dragging wheels or hitting walls, since on a real robot that would damage the sea floor and the machine.
  • Do it quickly and cheaply, since speed, turning radius, and parts cost were all graded.

Mechanical Design

  • Built a sturdy three-motor drivetrain, geared for a speed that balanced quick arena coverage against the fine control needed to stop accurately at the target.
  • Designed a simple claw to carry the object and release it onto the target, chosen over a more complex mechanism because it was reliable and easy to position.
  • Laid the chassis out for clean integration: the breadboard sits cleanly on top with the wiring accessible, and a status LED is clearly visible to show the robot's state.
  • Placed the sensors at the front, a proximity sensor for closing on the target and detecting obstacles, and IR sensors aimed to pick up the beacon.

Control Logic

The search-and-approach behaviour was written in RobotC. Rather than wander, the robot uses a rotate-and-sample strategy to turn the unknown target location into a heading it can drive toward, then refines that heading as it gets closer.

  1. Sweep for the signal. The robot turns in a full circle, sampling the IR sensor every 0.1 s. It stores the first reading as the maximum, then updates that maximum whenever a stronger reading comes in, so by the end of the turn it knows the strongest signal seen and the heading it came from.
  2. Lock onto the strongest heading. After the full circle, it keeps turning until it returns to that peak reading, which points it at the target.
  3. Drive in and re-check. It advances toward the beacon until it reaches a threshold distance, then repeats the circle-and-peak routine to correct for any drift and re-aim precisely at the target.
  4. Close to the placement standoff. It advances to a fixed 5 cm from the target, the distance that lines the claw up to set the object cleanly on top.
  5. Place and retreat. It releases the object onto the target, then backs away to clear the area and signal task completion.

Focus

Autonomous control, sensor integration, electromechanical assembly, drivetrain and gear-ratio selection, and design for a graded real-world task.
